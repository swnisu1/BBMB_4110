Model Discrimination: One or Two Exponentials

Simulate a decay with two rate constants, add noise, and fit it with a single and a double exponential. Move the rate constants together and watch what happens to RSS, AIC and BIC. RSS always improves with the bigger model; the information criteria say whether the improvement is worth the two extra parameters.

What happens as the rate constants approach each other?

Each point averages many simulated noise draws at one rate ratio (kfast/kslow), with everything else set by the sliders. Run it, then change a slider and run it again.

What to notice

  • RSS never gets worse. The double exponential contains the single one as a special case, so its RSS is always lower or equal, even when the data really are a single exponential. A lower RSS alone proves nothing.
  • ΔAIC and ΔBIC shrink toward zero as the rates converge. When the two rate constants differ by less than about 2 to 3 times, the second exponential is almost impossible to detect at realistic noise.
  • BIC is stricter than AIC. Its penalty per parameter is ln(n) instead of 2, so with more than about 8 points BIC asks for stronger evidence. When the truth is a single exponential, AIC picks the double more often by chance.
  • The fitted rate constants fall apart first. Before the criteria fail, the standard errors of kfast and kslow blow up and the amplitudes become large and opposite in sign. A double-exponential fit with huge uncertainties is not evidence of two phases.
  • The experiment matters as much as the analysis. Less noise, more points, and a time window that reaches several slow lifetimes all push the criteria toward the right answer. Try each slider.
